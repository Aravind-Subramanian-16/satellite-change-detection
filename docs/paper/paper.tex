\begin{abstract}
Deep change detectors are often trained on high-resolution aerial imagery such
as LEVIR-CD (0.5\,m), while Sentinel-2 provides 10\,m and 20\,m bands. This
paper reports a single-architecture case study of that gap. One MobileNetV2-based
Siamese U-Net is trained once on LEVIR-CD and frozen. At native resolution it
reaches pooled F1 87.06\%, IoU 77.08\%, precision 88.82\% and recall 85.36\% on
2,048 test pairs. We blur and downsample the same pairs to 1, 2, 4, 8 and 10\,m,
upsample them to $256\times256$, and re-evaluate without retraining. F1 is
70.81\% at 1\,m and 4.41\% at 2\,m; at 8--10\,m it is 2.61\%, recall is 1.32\%,
precision is about 99.5\%, and only 0.068\% of pixels are predicted changed.
Among 935 changed test images, 924 (98.8\%) receive no predicted change at
10\,m. The simulation isolates blur and resampling and does not reproduce
Sentinel-2 spectral, radiometric or atmospheric differences. We also evaluate
the frozen CNN zero-shot on the ten official OSCD test cities: pooled F1 is
0.0007 with joint-percentile preprocessing and 0.0000 with reflectance.
Train-selected spectral rules reach pooled F1 0.0782 (hardcoded NDVI/NDBI) and
0.1176 (percentile); Otsu predicts 67.35\% of pixels changed and is summarized
by overall accuracy 0.3439. These results do not establish spectral indices as
a validated alternative. The test cities had been scored more than once before
the reported run, so the evaluation is not an independent test of unseen
cities. Only one architecture and one training run are tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Automated change detection supports urban planning, disaster response and
land-cover monitoring. Much recent progress uses deep networks trained and
evaluated on high-resolution benchmarks such as LEVIR-CD, a dataset of
bitemporal Google Earth imagery with change annotations
\cite{chen2020stanet}. Results on a benchmark's native imagery do not by
themselves establish transfer to a different sensor, resolution or label
definition.

Sentinel-2 is a multispectral imaging mission with bands sampled at 10\,m,
20\,m and 60\,m spatial resolutions \cite{drusch2012sentinel,sentinel2mission}.
A 10\,m pixel is twenty times coarser linearly than a 0.5\,m aerial pixel. The
difference matters for small objects, mixed pixels and any decision that
assumes native spatial detail.

This paper reports an empirical case study, not a new detector. We take one
trained CNN, freeze it, and evaluate it in two ways. We apply controlled
blur and resampling to the LEVIR-CD test pairs to vary simulated resolution
while holding scene content fixed. We then evaluate the same frozen
checkpoint zero-shot on the official OSCD Sentinel-2 urban-change split and
compare it with train-selected spectral rules. The spectral rules are included
as inspectable reference points, not as a validated replacement for learned
detection. We report city-bootstrap intervals, trivial baselines and the prior
use of the test split so that the strength of each statement is visible.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a controlled degradation study of one frozen CNN over 2,048 LEVIR-CD
  test pairs at six simulated resolutions, with pooled confusion counts and a
  per-image characterization at 10\,m showing near-empty predictions rather than
  a rise in false positives;
  \item a zero-shot OSCD evaluation of the same CNN and train-selected
  NDVI/NDBI rules, with city-bootstrap intervals and trivial baselines; and
  \item an explicit disclosure that the OSCD test cities had been scored before
  the reported run, so the result is descriptive rather than an independent
  confirmation.
\end{itemize}
We do not claim a new architecture, a benchmark-leading score or a validated
operational system.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{CNN change detection.} Fully convolutional Siamese networks provide an
early end-to-end architecture for bitemporal change detection
\cite{daudt2018siamese}. STANet introduced spatial-temporal attention and the
LEVIR-CD dataset \cite{chen2020stanet}; BIT later used transformer-based
temporal feature fusion \cite{chen2022bit}. These benchmark results use
different protocols from the cross-resolution evaluation reported here and
are not treated as direct comparisons.

\textbf{Transfer and resolution shift.} Domain shift between training and
deployment imagery can involve changes in sensor, geography, season,
atmosphere and resolution. This paper isolates a simulated
blur-and-resampling factor in one experiment and reports a separate real-data
evaluation; it does not identify which factor causes the cross-dataset result.

\textbf{Spectral indices and thresholding.} NDVI is a vegetation index derived
from red and near-infrared reflectance \cite{rouse1974vegetation}. NDBI uses
shortwave-infrared and near-infrared reflectance to highlight built-up areas
\cite{zha2003ndbi}. At 10\,m, mixed pixels can dilute both indices, so an index
difference threshold is not resolution-independent. Otsu's method selects a
threshold from a gray-level histogram without labels \cite{otsu1979threshold}.

\textbf{Benchmarks and evaluation hygiene.} OSCD provides Sentinel-2
urban-change pairs and annotations for cross-city evaluation
\cite{daudt2018oscd}. We report the prior scoring history of its test cities
and do not present the reported run as a fresh, independent test. The
contribution is a bounded empirical case study, not a priority claim.

\section{Software Context and Scope}
The current repository contains a React/TypeScript browser application served
by an Express service. It compares provider RGB imagery and uploaded RGB pairs.
For uploaded pairs, users can select a normalized region of interest, inspect
the images with synchronized zoom and pan, and view a thresholded RGB
pixel-difference screen. Provider imagery may be a composite or mosaic rather
than an acquisition matching the requested date. If a complete provider pair
is unavailable, the application labels generated imagery as a demonstration.
The current application does not run the CNN or multispectral NDVI/NDBI
pipeline described in the supplied draft; its RGB results are screening
signals, not validated change classifications.

The experiments in Sections IV--V are separate historical offline evaluations
of a frozen CNN and spectral rules. They are not measurements of the current
browser application. The saved per-image degradation counts, per-city OSCD
confusion counts, and scripts for deriving summary metrics are included in the
repository history and have been restored. Their aggregate metrics and paper
tables were recomputed from those records for this revision. The original
imagery, model checkpoint and complete run environment are not available here,
so inference and the original evaluations could not be rerun. No claim about
application accuracy or operational performance is made. The distinction
between the historical experiments and the current software is essential to
interpreting this work.

\section{Controlled Resolution Degradation}
\subsection{Protocol}
The checkpoint is a MobileNetV2-based Siamese U-Net with a 3D-convolution
fusion module. It was trained once on $256\times256$ LEVIR-CD patches with a
combined Dice and binary cross-entropy loss, AdamW (learning rate
$5\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 4 and cosine annealing.
Training was configured for up to 100 epochs with early stopping (patience 10)
and stopped after 27 epochs; the best validation IoU was 0.7205. The checkpoint
was frozen for all experiments below.

We used the 2,048 LEVIR-CD test pairs. For degradation factor $f>1$, each image
was blurred with a PIL Gaussian filter of radius $f/2$ pixels, downsampled to
$(256/f)\times(256/f)$ with LANCZOS resampling, and upsampled back to
$256\times256$ with LANCZOS. At 10\,m this is a $12\times12$ intermediate for a
$256\times256$ input. Images were normalized with ImageNet statistics and passed
to the frozen CNN with a 0.5 probability threshold. TP, FP and FN were counted
per image, and Table~\ref{tab:degradation} pools the counts over all 2,048 pairs
at each level. The procedure holds scene content fixed and varies blur and
sampling; it does not reproduce Sentinel-2 spectral bands, radiometric
calibration, atmospheric correction, mixed-pixel composition or sensor noise.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:degradation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution} report the pooled
results. F1 is 87.06\% at 0.5\,m, falls to 70.81\% at 1\,m (a 16.25-point
absolute drop), and collapses to 4.41\% at 2\,m. It is 2.84\% at 4\,m and
2.61\% at 8--10\,m. Recall falls from 85.36\% at 0.5\,m to 57.01\% at 1\,m and
2.26\% at 2\,m, then 1.32\% at 8--10\,m. Precision is 88.82\%, 93.43\%,
90.78\%, 97.69\%, 99.59\% and 99.47\% across the six levels. The decline in F1
is therefore recall-dominated.

The high precision at coarse levels should not be read as useful detection. At
10\,m only 91,006 of 134,217,728 pixels are predicted changed (0.068\%), against
6,837,404 changed ground-truth pixels. Precision is computed from 90,527 true
positives and 479 false positives, so it describes how little the model predicts
rather than how well it localizes change.

\subsection{Prediction Behavior at 10\,m}
Of the 935 test images that contain ground-truth change, 924 (98.82\%) receive
no predicted change pixels at 10\,m. The remaining 11 divide into 4 with IoU
below 0.1, 5 between 0.1 and 0.5, and 2 at or above 0.5. These counts are
descriptive, not mechanistic; they do not separate resolution loss from the
other differences in the simulation.

\section{Real Sentinel-2 Evaluation on OSCD}
\subsection{Data and Protocol}
OSCD provides fourteen training cities and ten official test cities with
pixel-level urban-change labels \cite{daudt2018oscd}. We use five
bands aligned to the label grid: B02, B03, B04, B08 and B11. The pooled test set
contains 3,077,936 pixels, of which 5.17\% are labelled change. The benchmark
layout used here does not include the Sentinel-2 Scene Classification Layer, so
cloud and shadow exclusion is not evaluated.

Three spectral configurations were evaluated. The hardcoded rule flags a pixel
when NDVI drops by at least 0.30 or NDBI rises by at least 0.30. The percentile
rule thresholds the positive-change evidence at the 99.0th percentile. Otsu
thresholds the same evidence without labels \cite{otsu1979threshold}. The
hardcoded and percentile settings were selected on the fourteen training cities
over fixed grids according to the saved code and project records; the selected
hardcoded values lie at the edges of the search grid, while 99.0 is interior to
the percentile grid. The saved v2 path contains no test-label fitting step, but
the complete threshold-selection and run logs are unavailable, so this
separation cannot be independently audited.

The CNN is evaluated zero-shot with two preprocessing variants. Its input is an
RGB stack (B04, B03, B02). Reflectance scaling maps raw values to 0--255 by
dividing by 10,000; joint-percentile scaling uses the 2nd and 98th percentiles of
both dates combined. Scenes are processed in $256\times256$ tiles with 32-pixel
overlap and averaged probabilities. Joint-percentile was selected on training
cities; reflectance is the alternative.

\textbf{Scoring history.} The ten official test cities had been scored before
the reported v2 run. An earlier preliminary run with a smaller threshold grid
produced pooled F1 values of 0.167 (percentile), 0.104 (hardcoded), 0.095
(Otsu) and 0.0 (CNN). The total number of scoring events is not recorded. The v2
run re-evaluated those cities after the configuration was fixed on training
cities. It is therefore not an independent test of unseen cities, and we do not
present it as one.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:oscd} reports pooled results. The percentile rule has the highest
pooled F1 among the spectral configurations (0.1176, 95\% city-bootstrap CI
0.0540--0.1802), with precision 0.2419 and recall 0.0777. The hardcoded rule
reaches F1 0.0782 (CI 0.0168--0.1846) and predicts 2.24\% of pixels as changed
against a true 5.17\%. The paired difference in F1 between percentile and
hardcoded is $+0.0394$, with a bootstrap interval of $-0.0176$ to $+0.0778$;
the interval includes zero, so the ranking is not resolved at the 5\% level.
Both spectral rules under-predict change in aggregate. A random predictor at
5.17\% prevalence has F1 0.0517; only the percentile interval lies entirely
above it, narrowly.
Otsu predicts 67.35\% of pixels changed and obtains overall accuracy 0.3439,
with precision 0.0513 and recall 0.6684. Its F1 is 0.0953 (CI 0.0495--0.1245),
but its predicted fraction is so far above the true prevalence that F1 is not a
useful standalone summary; overall accuracy is reported as its headline score.

The CNN variants are close to empty. The joint-percentile variant predicts 0.01\%
of pixels and reaches F1 0.0007 (CI 0.0000--0.0017); the reflectance variant
predicts 0.00\% and reaches F1 0.0000. Their overall accuracy is 0.9483, equal to
the all-no-change accuracy on this test set. This is expected for a near-empty
predictor on a 5.17\% positive class and is not detection performance.

\textbf{Training-city robustness.} We also checked threshold stability without
touching the test split. For each of the 14 training cities, the hardcoded and
percentile configurations were selected on the other 13 training cities and
applied to the held-out training city. Pooled held-out F1 was 0.0976 for the
hardcoded rule (IoU 0.0513, precision 0.0838, recall 0.1168) and 0.1469 for the
percentile rule (IoU 0.0793, precision 0.1544, recall 0.1401). The hardcoded
selection sat on a search-grid edge in all 14 folds; the percentile selection
did not. The hardcoded scores are therefore limited by the grid. This is a
training-split robustness check, not a test-set score, and it adds no
test-scoring event.

\subsection{City-Level Variation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:oscd} shows that the pooled numbers hide large city differences.
Percentile F1 ranges from 0.0005 (saclay\_w) to 0.2481 (chongqing); hardcoded F1
ranges from 0.0032 (dubai) to 0.3102 (chongqing). The joint-percentile CNN
produces no predicted change in eight of ten cities and reaches at most 0.0024
(lasvegas); reflectance produces none. These
ranges are descriptive. The per-city intervals in Fig.~\ref{fig:oscd}(a)
resample pixels independently, ignoring spatial correlation; the pooled
intervals resample the ten cities. Neither includes model-training or
threshold-selection uncertainty.

The simulation isolates blur and resampling; the OSCD evaluation uses real data
but confounds resolution with spectral, radiometric, atmospheric and geographic
differences. No cloud or shadow mask is available in this OSCD layout, so cloud
effects are also uncontrolled.

\section{Discussion}
The sharp transition between 1\,m and 2\,m is consistent with the loss of spatial
information on which this architecture depends, but the experiment does not
identify which features cause it. The practical implication is narrower and
better supported: a model validated only at its native resolution can fail on
coarser imagery, and a native-resolution benchmark score does not reveal that
risk.

The coarse-resolution precision values illustrate a reporting problem. A
near-empty predictor can obtain high precision and high overall accuracy while
missing almost all change. Reporting F1 alone can also mislead when the
predicted fraction is extreme, as with Otsu. Precision, recall, predicted
fraction and true prevalence need to be read together, and overall accuracy is
uninformative when the positive class is small.

For decision support, outputs should be treated as candidate evidence requiring
human verification, not autonomous change polygons. The current browser
application exposes RGB difference screening and source limitations; it does
not produce validated land-cover classes or physical-area estimates. The
spectral rules are transparent but weak: the percentile rule improves
pooled F1 over the hardcoded rule, yet both under-predict change and vary widely
by city. Their results do not establish NDVI/NDBI differencing as a validated
low-resolution fallback. Otsu is not a drop-in fix; its high recall is bought
with very low precision on this class balance.

\section{Limitations}
The degradation experiment applies blur and resampling to LEVIR-CD rather than
acquiring real imagery at each resolution. It omits Sentinel-2 band differences,
radiometric calibration, atmospheric correction, mixed pixels and sensor noise.
OSCD supplies real 10\,m data, but the layout used here has ten test cities, no
SCL layer and urban labels only. Only one architecture and one training run were
tested; there was no retraining, fine-tuning, seed variation or architecture
comparison, and the training seed was not recorded. The hardcoded thresholds sat on a search-grid edge in all 14
training folds (Sec.~V-B), so the hardcoded scores are limited by the grid as
well as by the data. No decision-threshold tuning was tested. The
official test cities had been scored before the reported run, and the total
number of scoring events is not recorded. The saved per-image degradation CSV
and per-city OSCD confusion-count JSON are present, and their reported
aggregate metrics can be reproduced from those records. The original imagery,
model checkpoint and complete run environment are unavailable, so original
inference cannot be independently rerun. No confidence intervals are reported
for overall accuracy, and the per-city intervals use an independent-pixel
approximation. The reported benchmark evaluates a historical offline pipeline
and frozen checkpoint, not the current application's imagery retrieval, RGB
screening, exports or user interface.

All test-set scores and per-image counts in this paper come from two saved
files: \path{degradation_results.csv} and
\path{benchmarks/results/oscd_results_v2.json}. The training-only robustness
values come from \path{benchmarks/results/oscd_loco_thresholds.json}, produced
without reading a test label. Their SHA-256 hashes and derivation instructions
are recorded in the accompanying artifact guide. The figure/table generator
reads the saved result files only and records their hashes in a manifest; it
does not run model inference or rescore any test city for this paper.

\section{Conclusion}
We reported a single-architecture case study in which a CNN with 87.06\% F1 at
native 0.5\,m resolution falls to 70.81\% at 1\,m and 4.41\% at 2\,m under
blur-and-resampling, and to 2.61\% at 8--10\,m with near-empty predictions.
This specified blur-and-resampling simulation alone was sufficient to collapse
this detector; it does not reproduce all differences of real Sentinel-2 data.
A zero-shot OSCD evaluation of the same frozen CNN yields
pooled F1 0.0007 or lower. Train-selected NDVI/NDBI rules also perform poorly
(pooled F1 0.0782--0.1176), so the results do not establish spectral indices as
a validated alternative. The test cities had been scored before the reported
run. Resolution mismatch should be evaluated explicitly, and claims about
low-resolution fallbacks require real-data validation on independent cities.
